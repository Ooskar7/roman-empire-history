# Tarquinius Superbus

## Biography

Lucius Tarquinius Superbus, or Tarquin the Proud, was remembered as Rome seventh and final king. His ancestry connected him with an ambitious royal family of Etruscan background, but Roman tradition defined him above all through arrogance and illegitimate power.

Ancient accounts say he gained the throne after the death of Servius Tullius and ruled with little respect for the Senate. He completed major works and pursued war, but those achievements were overshadowed by his harshness and the crimes associated with his household.

## Character and Public Image

Tarquinius was made the negative image of Roman kingship. He was proud, suspicious, and unwilling to accept limits. Later Republican writers used his character as a warning that monarchy naturally tended toward domination when public authority became private possession.

The ancient evidence for Tarquinius Superbus is uneven. Some details come from writers who lived much later, and some portraits were shaped by political admiration, moral judgment, or hostility. For that reason, this page treats the figure not only as a list of actions, but as a person remembered through Roman historical imagination. The dates usually given for this life are Unknown to 495 BC, but for legendary or poorly documented figures the chronology should be read as traditional rather than certain.

## Historical Importance

His expulsion gave Romans one of their strongest political memories. Even centuries later, accusations of wanting to be king carried the shadow of Tarquinius. His biography mattered less as private life than as a symbol of the tyranny the Republic claimed to reject.

Tarquinius Superbus is important to Roman history because the figure connects personal ambition, public duty, and the wider pressures of the age. The related events explain the battles, reforms, crises, or religious changes in more detail; this biography focuses on the person behind those developments, the social world that formed them, and the reputation they left behind. Through that reputation, later generations learned what Romans admired, feared, condemned, or hoped to preserve.
