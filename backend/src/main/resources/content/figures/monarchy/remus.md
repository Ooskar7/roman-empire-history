# Remus

## The Twin Brother of Romulus

**Remus** was the legendary twin brother of **Romulus** and one of the central figures in the foundation myth of Rome. Although Romulus ultimately became the founder and first king of the city, Remus played an essential role in the story of Rome’s origins and became a symbolic figure associated with rivalry, tragedy, and the violent birth of Roman civilization.

In Roman tradition, the story of Remus was inseparable from that of Romulus. Together, the brothers represented the divine and heroic origins of Rome, while their final conflict symbolized the internal struggles and sacrifices that Romans believed accompanied the rise of their city.

## Birth and Divine Origins

According to Roman mythology, Remus and Romulus were born in **Alba Longa**, an ancient Latin city traditionally considered the ancestor-city of Rome. Their mother was **Rhea Silvia**, daughter of the rightful king **Numitor**. After Numitor had been overthrown by his brother **Amulius**, Rhea Silvia was forced to become a Vestal Virgin in order to prevent her from having children who could challenge the usurper’s power.

Despite this, she gave birth to the twins. Roman tradition claimed that the god **Mars**, the god of war, was their father. This divine ancestry connected the future city of Rome to military strength and the favor of the gods from its earliest beginnings.

Fearing the threat posed by the children, Amulius ordered the twins to be abandoned near the River Tiber. However, they survived and were famously discovered by a **she-wolf**, which cared for them in the cave known as the *Lupercal*. Eventually, they were rescued and raised by the shepherd **Faustulus** and his wife.

The image of the she-wolf nursing Romulus and Remus became one of the most recognizable symbols of Roman civilization.

## Revenge Against Amulius

As they grew older, Remus and Romulus became respected leaders among local shepherds and rural communities. Eventually, they discovered the truth about their birth and lineage.

Determined to restore justice, the twins returned to Alba Longa and overthrew Amulius, restoring their grandfather Numitor to the throne. This victory established them as heroic figures and marked the beginning of their destiny to create a new city.

After restoring Numitor, the brothers chose to found a settlement near the place where they had survived as infants. However, disagreements quickly emerged between them regarding the location and leadership of the new city.

## The Conflict Between the Brothers

The most famous and tragic episode involving Remus was his conflict with Romulus during the founding of Rome.

According to Roman tradition, Remus preferred the **Aventine Hill**, while Romulus favored the **Palatine Hill**. To determine which brother had divine approval, they consulted the gods through augury, observing the flight of birds in the sky. Both brothers claimed victory, increasing tensions between them.

The dispute eventually turned violent. In the most widely known version of the myth, Remus mocked the walls that Romulus was building around the city and jumped over them to demonstrate their weakness. Romulus reacted furiously and killed his brother, declaring that the same fate would await anyone who crossed the walls of Rome.

The death of Remus became one of the defining moments of Roman mythology.

## Historical and Cultural Meaning

Modern historians consider the story of Remus legendary, yet it carried deep symbolic meaning for Roman culture.

Remus represented the tragic cost of Rome’s foundation and the idea that conflict and sacrifice were inseparable from the rise of great powers. The rivalry between the brothers also reflected themes that repeatedly appeared throughout Roman history: competition for power, civil violence, and the tension between personal loyalty and political destiny.

For the Romans, the story of Remus was not simply a myth about a lost brother. It was a reminder that Rome itself had been born through struggle, ambition, and bloodshed.

Even though Rome took its name from Romulus, the memory of Remus remained permanently woven into the identity and mythology of the Roman world.
