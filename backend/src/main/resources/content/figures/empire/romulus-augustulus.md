# Romulus Augustulus

## Biography

Romulus Augustulus was placed on the western throne by his father Orestes, a military commander. His name joined Rome legendary founder with the title of Augustus, but his political reality was small and fragile.

He ruled only briefly and mostly as a figurehead. Real power belonged to Orestes and the army. When Odoacer revolted, Orestes was killed and Romulus was deposed rather than treated as a major enemy.

## Character and Public Image

Because he was young and powerless, Romulus Augustulus is remembered more as symbol than actor. Ancient and later writers saw irony in his name: Romulus at the beginning of Rome, Romulus at the end of the western empire.

The ancient evidence for Romulus Augustulus is uneven. Some details come from writers who lived much later, and some portraits were shaped by political admiration, moral judgment, or hostility. For that reason, this page treats the figure not only as a list of actions, but as a person remembered through Roman historical imagination. The dates usually given for this life are 461 AD to Unknown, but for legendary or poorly documented figures the chronology should be read as traditional rather than certain.

## Historical Importance

His deposition became the traditional date for the fall of the Western Roman Empire. His life reminds us that historical turning points can center on people with little personal power, because institutions around them have already collapsed.

Romulus Augustulus is important to Roman history because the figure connects personal ambition, public duty, and the wider pressures of the age. The related events explain the battles, reforms, crises, or religious changes in more detail; this biography focuses on the person behind those developments, the social world that formed them, and the reputation they left behind. Through that reputation, later generations learned what Romans admired, feared, condemned, or hoped to preserve.

For readers moving through the Roman timeline, Romulus Augustulus works best as a biographical anchor rather than only as a name attached to an event. The figure helps show how individual choices, reputation, and social position could shape the larger movement of Roman history.
