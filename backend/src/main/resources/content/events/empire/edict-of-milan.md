# The Edict of Milan (313 AD)

## Legal Toleration for Christianity

In 313 AD, **Constantine** and **Licinius** agreed on a policy of religious toleration commonly known as the **Edict of Milan**. It granted Christians and others the freedom to worship and ordered the return of confiscated Christian property.

The agreement followed the end of the Great Persecution, launched under Diocletian and continued by some of his successors. That persecution had attempted to force Christians back into traditional religious conformity through arrests, destruction of churches, surrender of scriptures, and pressure to sacrifice to the Roman gods.

## A New Imperial Policy

The Edict of Milan did not make Christianity the official religion of the empire. Instead, it made Christianity legal and protected. This was still a dramatic change. A religion that had recently been targeted by imperial power now received recognition from emperors.

Constantine's western victory at the Milvian Bridge in 312 AD gave him the authority to support Christians openly. Licinius, ruling in the East, joined the agreement at Milan. The policy helped stabilize religious conflict and aligned imperial rule with a broader claim of divine favor and public peace.

For Christian communities, the consequences were immediate. Confiscated meeting places and property could be restored. Bishops could act more publicly. Churches could be built with imperial support. Christian leaders increasingly became visible figures in civic life.

## Limits and Tensions

Religious toleration did not end conflict. Christians disagreed among themselves over doctrine and authority, and emperors soon became involved in those disputes. Traditional cults also continued, and many inhabitants of the empire remained non-Christian for generations.

The edict therefore began a transition rather than completing one. The Roman state moved from persecuting Christianity to favoring it, but the relationship between church and empire still had to be defined.

## Historical Meaning

The Edict of Milan was one of the major turning points in Roman religious history. It showed that imperial power could support religious pluralism when doing so served political stability, but it also opened the way for Christianity to become closely connected with the state.

In the long view, 313 AD marked the beginning of Christianity's public rise within Roman institutions. The faith moved from margins and occasional persecution toward patronage, councils, basilicas, and imperial protection. Later emperors would go much further, but the legal transformation began here.
