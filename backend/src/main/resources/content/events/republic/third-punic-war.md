# The Third Punic War and the Destruction of Carthage (146 BC)

## The Final War with Carthage

The **Third Punic War** ended the long rivalry between Rome and Carthage. By the mid-second century BC, Carthage was no longer the great military threat it had been under Hannibal. It had lost its empire, paid indemnities, and lived under restrictions imposed by Rome. Yet many Romans continued to fear its recovery.

Roman politics turned increasingly hostile toward Carthage. The senator **Cato the Elder** famously argued that Carthage must be destroyed. The immediate cause of war came from conflict between Carthage and the Numidian king Masinissa, a Roman ally. When Carthage acted militarily without Roman permission, Rome treated it as a violation of the peace.

## Siege and Destruction

Roman demands became impossible for Carthage to accept, including the abandonment of the city itself. The Carthaginians chose resistance. From 149 to 146 BC, Rome besieged Carthage in a brutal and determined campaign.

The final assault was led by **Scipio Aemilianus**, the adopted grandson of Scipio Africanus. Roman troops entered the city street by street. Ancient accounts describe desperate fighting, fires, starvation, and the collapse of one of the great cities of the Mediterranean world.

In 146 BC, Carthage was destroyed. Survivors were enslaved, and the territory became the Roman province of Africa. Later legends claimed that Rome sowed the ground with salt, though this detail is not considered historical.

## Historical Meaning

The destruction of Carthage showed the harsh side of Roman power. Rome no longer merely defeated rival states; it could erase them as independent political communities. The same year, Rome also destroyed Corinth in Greece, making 146 BC a symbol of Mediterranean domination.

For Romans, the end of Carthage removed an old enemy and avenged the terror of Hannibal's invasion. For modern historians, it also reveals how fear, ambition, and imperial habit could combine. Carthage was dangerous in Roman memory even when it was much weaker in reality.

The Third Punic War confirmed that Rome had become the supreme power of the western Mediterranean. Yet victory carried moral and political consequences. Wealth from empire grew, provincial administration expanded, and Roman elites competed for glory on a larger stage. The Republic had defeated its greatest rival, but its own internal conflicts were becoming harder to control.
