# The Assassination of King Servius Tullius (535 BC)

## The Fall of a Reforming King

According to Roman tradition, **Servius Tullius**, the sixth king of Rome, was assassinated in 535 BC through a conspiracy organized by his own daughter, **Tullia**, and her husband, **Lucius Tarquinius Superbus**, later known as Tarquin the Proud.

The event became one of the darkest and most dramatic episodes of the Roman monarchy. Ancient Roman historians described it as a symbol of corruption, ambition, and the collapse of lawful authority that would eventually lead to the downfall of the Roman kingship itself.

Servius Tullius had ruled Rome during a period of important political and social reforms. Roman tradition credited him with reorganizing Roman society through the **census system**, dividing citizens according to wealth and military obligation rather than purely by birth. He also expanded the city and strengthened its defenses, most famously through the construction of the **Servian Wall**, which later became associated with the protection of Rome.

Despite these achievements, Servius faced growing opposition among parts of the Roman aristocracy, especially from the ambitious Lucius Tarquinius.

## The Ambition of Tullia and Tarquinius

The story of the conspiracy was remembered by Roman writers as a tale of ruthless political ambition. Servius Tullius had two daughters, both named Tullia, while Tarquinius and his brother Arruns Tarquinius were married to them. According to the legend, the younger Tullia and Lucius Tarquinius shared a desire for power and despised their more moderate spouses.

Roman tradition claimed that the ambitious pair arranged the deaths of their respective husband and wife, allowing them to marry each other and pursue the throne together. Ancient historians portrayed Tullia as exceptionally cruel and determined, encouraging Tarquinius to seize power directly.

Eventually, Lucius Tarquinius entered the Roman Senate surrounded by armed supporters and declared himself king. When Servius Tullius confronted him, Tarquinius violently threw the elderly ruler down the steps of the senate house.

Although Servius survived the fall, assassins sent by Tarquinius murdered him in the streets of Rome.

## Tullia’s Infamous Ride

The most infamous part of the story came immediately afterward. According to Roman legend, Tullia drove her chariot through the streets of Rome and encountered the body of her murdered father lying in the road.

Rather than stopping, she ordered the driver to continue forward, and the chariot passed over Servius Tullius’ corpse. Roman tradition claimed that the street later became known as the **Vicus Sceleratus**, the “Street of Crime,” because of this horrifying act.

Ancient Roman historians used this scene as a powerful moral symbol of unnatural ambition, impiety, and the destruction of family loyalty in the pursuit of political power.

## The Rise of Tarquinius Superbus

After the assassination, Lucius Tarquinius Superbus became the seventh and final king of Rome. Unlike many earlier kings, he was portrayed as a tyrant who ruled through fear, violence, and personal authority rather than respect for Roman customs and institutions.

Roman writers described his reign as oppressive and arrogant, emphasizing his refusal to consult the Senate and his reliance on executions and intimidation. These traditions later became central to the Roman explanation for why the monarchy eventually collapsed.

## Historical and Cultural Meaning

Modern historians consider much of this story legendary or heavily dramatized, but it held enormous symbolic importance in Roman culture.

The assassination of Servius Tullius represented the corruption of the Roman monarchy and the dangers of unchecked ambition. The brutality of Tullia and Tarquinius became moral examples used by later Romans to condemn tyranny and political violence.

The event also served as a direct prelude to the final crisis of the monarchy. Roman tradition viewed the rise of Tarquinius Superbus as the beginning of the tyranny that would ultimately provoke the revolution of 509 BC and the creation of the Roman Republic.
