# The Reigns of Numa Pompilius and Tullus Hostilius (715 - 640 BC)

## The Successors of Romulus

After the death of **Romulus**, Roman tradition states that the throne passed to **Numa Pompilius**, the second king of Rome, whose reign contrasted sharply with that of the city’s warrior founder. While Romulus represented conquest and military strength, Numa became associated with peace, religion, and the organization of Roman institutions.

Following Numa’s reign, the kingship passed to **Tullus Hostilius**, the third king of Rome. In Roman memory, Tullus represented a return to military expansion and warfare. Together, the reigns of these two rulers symbolized the dual nature of early Rome: a civilization shaped both by religious order and military ambition.

## Numa Pompilius and the Religious Foundations of Rome

According to Roman tradition, Numa Pompilius was a wise Sabine ruler chosen for his justice and moderation. Unlike Romulus, he was said to dislike war and instead focused on establishing laws, rituals, and religious customs that would define Roman society for centuries.

Ancient sources credited Numa with creating many of Rome’s earliest religious institutions. He organized priesthoods, regulated sacred ceremonies, and established important religious offices such as the **Pontifex Maximus**, the chief priest of Rome. He also founded the order of the **Vestal Virgins**, priestesses responsible for maintaining the sacred eternal flame of the city.

Numa was traditionally associated with the construction of temples and sacred spaces, as well as the reform of the Roman calendar. Roman historians portrayed him as a ruler who brought stability and divine favor to the young city after the violent reign of Romulus.

Although much of the story is legendary, Numa became an enduring symbol of wisdom, piety, and lawful government in Roman culture.

## Tullus Hostilius and the Destruction of Alba Longa

After Numa’s peaceful reign, Rome was ruled by Tullus Hostilius, who was remembered as a far more aggressive and warlike king. Roman tradition describes him as eager to expand Roman power and restore the military spirit established by Romulus.

The most famous event of his reign was the conflict with **Alba Longa**, the ancient Latin city traditionally considered the mother-city of Rome and the birthplace of Romulus and Remus.

To avoid a full-scale war, the two cities supposedly agreed to settle their dispute through combat between champions. Rome was represented by the **Horatii** brothers, while Alba Longa was represented by the **Curiatii** brothers. According to legend, the Romans emerged victorious after a dramatic battle.

Despite the agreement, the ruler of Alba Longa later betrayed Rome during another conflict. Tullus Hostilius responded harshly: Alba Longa was destroyed, its population was transferred to Rome, and the city was permanently annexed into Roman territory.

This event held enormous symbolic importance in Roman tradition because it represented Rome’s rise to dominance among the Latin communities of central Italy. The annexation of Alba Longa also reinforced the idea that Rome inherited and absorbed the legacy of older Latin civilizations.

## The Curia Hostilia and the Roman Senate

Tullus Hostilius was also traditionally credited with constructing the **Curia Hostilia**, considered the first formal senate house of Rome. The building became the meeting place of the Roman Senate and an important center of political life.

Although later rebuilt and modified several times, the Curia Hostilia symbolized the growing political organization of the Roman state. It reflected the increasing importance of the Senate and the development of institutions that would later shape the Roman Republic.

## Historical and Cultural Meaning

Modern historians consider many details of these reigns to be legendary, yet the stories carried deep meaning for Roman identity.

Numa Pompilius represented religion, law, and civic order, while Tullus Hostilius symbolized conquest, discipline, and expansion. Together, they embodied the balance Romans believed was necessary for the greatness of their civilization: spiritual authority combined with military power.

The destruction of Alba Longa and the creation of the Curia Hostilia also reflected Rome’s transformation from a small settlement into a growing regional power with increasingly complex political institutions.
